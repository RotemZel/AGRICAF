How to Read the Explanations

Every AGRICAF forecast comes with a set of charts showing what stood behind it. They are built for people who want the reasoning, not the mathematics, and nothing on this page assumes you have any. If you read only one thing, read the last section.

What the forecast itself shows

The chart at the top of each commodity page runs from the past into the future. The black line is what the price actually did. The coloured line is what AGRICAF expects it to do over the coming year. The shaded band around it is the room for error. It widens the further ahead you look, because the further ahead you look, the less anyone knows.

Prices here are always given as a change against the same month one year earlier. Zero means the price is where it was a year ago. Plus twenty means it is twenty per cent higher.

The band is built to hold the outcome about four times in five. Checked against the record it has managed closer to seven times in ten, and the misses bunch together in the most turbulent years, which are the ones you would most want it to cover. Read it as a guide to how uncertain the number is, not as a promise.

The four questions

The explanation charts answer four different questions. They go from the widest view to the narrowest, so start at the top and go down only as far as you need.

What usually moves this price? Everything at once: every month of the year, and every distance ahead. Use it to see which handful of things do most of the work.
What moved it in a particular month? Narrows to one calendar month. Use it to see whether something matters every year, or only in unusual ones.
What happens as one thing changes? Follows a single factor. Use it to see whether a small change matters, or only a large one.
What only matters in combination? Some things matter on their own. Others only when they happen at the same time as something else. This is the view that separates the two.

The controls

Two rows of buttons sit beside most charts. The row marked H1 to H12 is how far ahead the forecast was made: H3 means the forecast was made three months before the month it describes. The row of single letters is the month being forecast, from January to December. Switch any of them off to clear the view.

Reading the colours

There are two views, and colour means something different in each. Check which one you are looking at before reading the colours.

“Relative Intensity”: how much did this factor matter, compared with the others?

01
intensity = size of this factor's effect ÷ size of every factor's effect added up

It runs from 0 to 1 and the column adds up to 1. An intensity of 0.35 means this factor accounted for 35% of everything the model used for that forecast, whichever way it pushed.

“Impact”: which way did this factor push the forecast, given the value it took?

strongly negative0strongly positive
impact = whether the factor rose or fell × its effect

When the factor's value and its effect carry the same sign, the impact is positive. When their signs differ, it is negative. The scale runs outwards from zero in the middle: the darker the colour, the stronger the impact, and a pale square means the factor barely moved the forecast either way. A factor that fell while the price rose, and a factor that rose while the price fell, are both the same kind of inverse relationship and both show blue.

On either view, a plain beige square means this factor was not used for that month and distance at all. It is an absence, not a low score.

Reading the names

Every factor is written as a change against the same month a year earlier, and the names follow one pattern.

Wheat price, 1 month agoThe crop's own price, one month before the forecast was made. Prices carry their own momentum.
USA stocksHow much the United States had in store, against a year earlier.
Argentina productionHow much Argentina grew, against a year earlier. Some pages say yield instead, which is how much per hectare.
Oil price, 12 months agoAnother commodity's price. Oil matters because it moves the cost of growing and shipping.
China exchange rate, 2 months agoA currency against the US dollar. Commodities are priced in dollars, so currencies change what a buyer really pays.
… , year beforeThe same figure, one year further back again.

What these charts do not say

These charts show what the forecast leaned on. They do not show what causes prices. When two things tend to move together, such as a currency and an oil price, the method cannot tell which of them the forecast was really following, and it may credit either one. So a factor near the top is a good place to start asking questions. It is not an answer, and it is not evidence that changing that factor would change the price.

One more honest limit. AGRICAF predicts the direction of a price move considerably better than its size, and the gap grows the further ahead you look. Inside about four months the size is worth reading. Beyond about eight months, treat the direction as the useful part and the exact number as a rough guide.
