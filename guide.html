Reading the AGRICAF Explanations

AGRICAF publishes twelve-month-ahead price forecasts for five internationally traded agricultural commodities, and accompanies each forecast with a record of the predictors the published model relied on to produce it. This page sets out how that record is computed and how it should be read.

About the tool and its methodology

Every commodity page carries two distinct products. The first is a forecast of the price change over the coming twelve months. The second is a set of diagnostics describing the behaviour of the model that produced it. The diagnostics are the subject of this guide.

AGRICAF fits a panel of candidate specifications for every combination of commodity, calendar month and forecast horizon. Each combination is treated as a separate estimation problem, and the specification published for that combination is the one with the lowest leave-one-year-out absolute error. Because the winning specification differs across combinations, the explanation layer is model-agnostic by necessity: it treats the published model as a black box and probes its behaviour rather than reading its coefficients.

Four post-hoc methods are applied, all through the iml package. Permutation importance measures the loss in accuracy when a predictor is shuffled. Shapley values allocate the deviation of a single prediction from the average prediction across the predictors that produced it. LIME fits a local surrogate around each prediction and is reported alongside the Shapley results as a cross-check rather than as a substitute. Friedman's H-statistic quantifies pairwise interaction. Formal definitions are given in the sections below, and sources in the references.

Two properties of the procedure are worth stating before any figure is read. First, the explanations are generated on a rolling basis, and no explanation is computed on the year it explains: each year's attribution comes from a model fitted only on earlier years. Second, several forecasting families in the AGRICAF panel, the time-series specifications and the recurrent networks among them, expose no learner that these methods can interrogate. Where one of those families is published for a combination, the best explainable specification for the same combination is explained in its place. The diagnostics then describe a close relative of the published forecast rather than the published forecast itself.

The forecast and its interval

The chart at the top of each commodity page runs from the historical record into the forecast period. The solid dark line is the realised price change. The coloured line is the central forecast over the coming twelve months. The shaded band is the prediction interval, and it widens with the horizon.

All prices are expressed as a change against the same calendar month one year earlier. A value of zero means the price is level with a year ago; a value of twenty means it is twenty per cent higher. This convention removes the seasonal pattern that would otherwise dominate a monthly agricultural series, and it is the quantity the models are estimated on.

The band is constructed for eighty per cent coverage. Evaluated against the historical record it has achieved closer to seventy per cent, and the shortfall is concentrated in the most volatile years, which are precisely the years in which an interval matters most. The band should therefore be read as an indication of how uncertain the central number is, and not as a calibrated guarantee.

Levels of explanation

The diagnostics are organised into levels of increasing granularity. A user scanning for early-warning signals needs a different view from one investigating a particular episode, and each level draws on a different aggregation of the same underlying quantities.

Level 1. Global overview Aggregates across all forecast years to produce one value per predictor for each month-and-horizon cell. The result is the typical attribution rather than the attribution in any one year. Answers questions of the form: which predictors drive wheat prices in general?
Level 2. Month and horizon specific Fixes one calendar month and one or more horizons, and retains the individual values from each forecast year instead of collapsing them to a mean. This preserves the year-to-year variability that Level 1 averages away, and so distinguishes a predictor that matters consistently from one that matters only in unusual years. Answers questions of the form: what drives the three-month-ahead maize forecast made each September, and how stable are those drivers?
Level 3. Predictor specific Follows a single predictor for a chosen month and horizon, pairing each historical observation of that predictor with its attribution. The resulting mapping exposes non-linearity and threshold effects: a moderate production shortfall may carry little price consequence while a large one carries a disproportionate consequence. Answers questions of the form: how do changes in Brazilian soybean production affect the six-month-ahead price forecast?

Reading the colour scales

Two colour scales appear across the diagnostics and they encode different quantities. Establish which scale a figure uses before reading it.

Relative Intensity

Relative Intensity measures how much of a forecast's total explanatory magnitude a given predictor accounts for. Writing ak,t for the attribution of predictor k at observation t, across K predictors:

intensityk,t = |ak,t| ÷ Σj=1..K |aj,t|
01

Intensity runs from 0 to 1 and sums to 1 across the predictors of a given forecast. An intensity of 0.35 means that predictor accounted for thirty-five per cent of the model's total explanatory magnitude for that forecast, regardless of direction. Because the measure is taken on absolute values, it is silent on whether the predictor raised or lowered the forecast.

The share construction has a direct consequence for interpretation. Since the shares sum to one, a single predictor at the red end of the scale implies that every other predictor sits near the green end. A column of uniformly moderate values is not possible for more than two or three predictors at once. Concentration is therefore visible at a glance, and a concentrated column is a signal that the forecast rests on a narrow base.

Impact

Impact combines the attribution with the direction of the predictor's own movement, so that the sign reports the direction of the estimated relationship rather than the direction of the contribution alone:

impactk,t = sign(xk,t) · ak,t
strongly negative0strongly positive

The interpretation is joint. Where the predictor value and its attribution carry the same sign, the impact is positive. Where their signs differ, it is negative. A predictor that fell while pushing the forecast up, and one that rose while pushing the forecast down, both describe an inverse relationship and both appear blue. The scale runs outwards from zero at the centre: the darker the colour, the stronger the impact, and a pale cell indicates a predictor that moved the forecast very little in either direction.

On either scale, a neutral beige cell indicates that the predictor was not selected for that month and horizon. It records an absence, not a low value.

Reading the interaction diagram

The interaction diagram is a hierarchical edge bundling layout. Each node is a predictor, coloured by type, with the four types labelled around the perimeter. An edge between two nodes carries Friedman's H-statistic for that pair, averaged over the explained years.

H is computed from the model's partial dependence surfaces. For predictors j and k, it is the share of the variance of their two-way surface that cannot be reproduced by adding their two one-way effects. It runs from 0, where the pair acts additively and the model's response to one predictor does not depend on the level of the other, to 1, where the pair's effect is entirely joint.

Computing H for every pair is quadratic in the reference sample, so AGRICAF screens the candidates: a predictor is paired against the rest only if it holds at least two per cent of that combination's permutation importance, averaged over the explained years. Predictors below the threshold do not appear as anchors.

H is normalised by the pair's own joint effect, not by the model's total. A high value therefore identifies a relationship that is largely interactive; it does not identify a pair that is influential in absolute terms. Two predictors of minor overall importance can record a high H because the denominator is their own small surface. Read the diagram alongside the intensity figures rather than in place of them. Note also that edge thickness is rescaled to the range currently displayed, so thickness is comparable within a view but not across views.

Reading the predictor names

Every predictor is expressed as a change against the same calendar month one year earlier, and the names follow a single convention. A suffix in brackets gives the lag at which the predictor enters the model.

Wheat price (1m)The commodity's own price, lagged one month relative to the point of forecast.
Oil price (12m)Another commodity's price at the stated lag. Energy prices enter through production and freight costs.
Fertilizer index (3m)A price index at the stated lag.
Brazil FX (6m)A bilateral exchange rate against the US dollar. Internationally traded commodities are quoted in dollars, so the currency alters the price faced by a buyer or received by a producer.
USA stocksClosing stocks held by the stated region, against a year earlier.
Argentina prod.Production by the stated region, against a year earlier. Some commodities are modelled on yield instead, which is output per hectare.
… (prev)The same quantity taken one further year back.

Limitations

The diagnostics describe the behaviour of a fitted model. They are not estimates of an economic structure, and the following constraints apply to any use of them.

  1. Attribution is not identification. These figures report what the forecast leaned on, not what moves prices. Where two predictors move together, as an exchange rate and an energy price commonly do, the method cannot determine which the model was following and may credit either. A predictor near the top of a ranking is a reasonable place to begin an investigation, not the conclusion of one.
  2. Correlated predictors weaken the attribution itself. The standard Shapley estimator assumes predictors can be varied independently. Where they cannot, attributions are distributed across correlated predictors in a way that need not reflect the model's actual dependence (Aas et al., 2021).
  3. Permutation-based measures evaluate the model off the observed data. Shuffling a predictor creates combinations that never occurred, so the model is scored in regions it was never fitted on (Hooker et al., 2021).
  4. Post-hoc explanations carry their own uncertainty. Sampling-based attributions vary between runs and that variation is rarely reported (Slack et al., 2021). For decisions with material consequences, Rudin (2019) argues for models that are interpretable by construction rather than explained after the fact.
  5. Where the published specification is not explainable, a stand-in is explained. See the methodology section above. The diagnostics then characterise a comparable model, not the published one.
  6. Direction is forecast more reliably than magnitude, and the gap widens with the horizon. Within roughly four months the magnitude carries information. Beyond roughly eight months, the direction is the usable part and the point estimate should be treated as indicative.
